#### Mandatory "No Double-Dipping" Assessment Rule

> **HARD CONSTRAINT IN APP WORK REGISTRY:** Once a literary work (or any part of a grouped work) is assigned to one assessment component among **{Individual Oral, HL Essay, Paper 2}**, **the entire work becomes permanently locked and unavailable for the other two components**.
> - Minimum distinct works consumed across HL assessments: **5 works** (`2 for IO` + `1 for HL Essay` + `2 for Paper 2`).
> - IB best practice: Prepare **3 works** for Paper 2 (not 2) so at least one pair cleanly fits 1 of the 4 unseen exam prompts — bringing recommended active exam-prep works to **6** (`2 IO + 1 HLE + 3 P2`), leaving 4 works (in the 10-work model) as foundational AoE / close-reading texts.

### 2.1 The 3 Areas of Exploration (AoE)

Every HL student must study **at least 3 works** in each Area of Exploration. The three AoEs are overlapping lenses, not isolated silos.

#### Area 1: Readers, Writers and Texts
* **Core Focus:** Close reading, how literary language and form construct meaning, authorial choices, aesthetic quality, and how readers actively negotiate and interpret texts. (Primary engine for **Paper 1** and close textual analysis across all components.)
* **Official Guiding Conceptual Questions (6):**
  1. Why and how do we study literature?
  2. How are we affected by literary texts in various ways?
  3. In what ways is meaning constructed, negotiated, expressed and interpreted?
  4. How does language use vary among literary forms?
  5. How does the structure or style of a literary text affect meaning?
  6. How do literary texts offer both insights and challenges?
* **TOK Links:** Is literary meaning fixed by authorial intent or co-constructed by readers? What criteria make one textual interpretation more valid or persuasive than another? How does specialized literary language produce knowledge differently from scientific or historical prose?
* **Learner Portfolio Activity Mapping:**
  - Unseen 45-minute close-reading annotations on short extracts
  - "Effect vs. Device" transformation rewrites on key passages
  - Creative pastiche/imitation tasks (e.g., rewriting a scene in a different focalization or poetic form followed by an analytical rationale)
  - Reader-response log tracking how initial impressions shift on a second reading

#### Area 2: Time and Space
* **Core Focus:** Cultural, historical, political, and geographical contexts of both **production** (the world the writer inhabited) and **reception** (how diverse audiences across eras and cultures read the text). Essential for **works in translation** and **Individual Oral** global-to-local framing.
* **Official Guiding Conceptual Questions (6):**
  1. How important is cultural or historical context to the production and reception of a text?
  2. How do we approach texts from different time periods and cultures to our own?
  3. To what extent do texts offer insights into another culture?
  4. How does the meaning and impact of a text change over time?
  5. How do texts reflect, represent, or form a part of cultural practices?
  6. How does language represent social distinctions and identities?
* **TOK Links:** To what extent can a modern reader truly understand a text produced in a radically different historical/cultural framework? Does translation preserve or alter knowledge? How do ideological biases shape reception across eras?
* **Learner Portfolio Activity Mapping:**
  - Context Dossiers (mapping historical events, publishing censorship, gender norms, or colonial structures onto specific chapters/acts)
  - Translation Comparison Labs (comparing two English translations of the same stanza/scene — e.g., Fagles vs. Wilson for *Antigone* or *The Odyssey*, or Meyer vs. McFarlane for *A Doll's House* — analyzing shifts in register, tone, and rhythm)
  - Global Issue Extraction Logs linking local cultural practices in a text to transnational phenomena
  - Reception Timelines (comparing contemporary reviews with 21st-century feminist, postcolonial, or ecocritical readings)

#### Area 3: Intertextuality: Connecting Texts
* **Core Focus:** How texts converse with, echo, parody, adapt, or subvert other texts and genre conventions; comparative synthesis across eras, cultures, and forms. (Primary engine for **Paper 2** and **Individual Oral** cross-text pairing.)
* **Official Guiding Conceptual Questions (6):**
  1. How do texts adhere to and deviate from conventions associated with literary forms or text types?
  2. How do conventions and systems of reference evolve over time?
  3. In what ways can diverse texts share points of similarity?
  4. How valid is the notion of a classic text?
  5. How can texts offer multiple perspectives of a single issue, topic or theme?
  6. In what ways can comparison and interpretation be transformative?
* **TOK Links:** Does placing two texts side-by-side generate new interpretive knowledge that neither text produces alone? Who decides what constitutes the literary "canon" or a "classic," and what knowledge/voices are excluded by canon formation?
* **Learner Portfolio Activity Mapping:**
  - Comparative Matrices (2- or 3-text synthesis tables across themes, endings, narrative voices, and structural motifs)
  - Allusion & Archetype Tracking (mapping biblical, mythic, Shakespearean, or folkloric echoes inside modern texts)
  - Genre Subversion Audits (comparing how two works break traditional rules of tragedy, bildungsroman, or sonnet form)
  - Mock Paper 2 Comparative Thesis Builders pairing unlikely texts (e.g., *Antigone* + *Persepolis*, *Hamlet* + *Beloved*)

#### Learner Portfolio Requirements (Course Backbone)
* **Status:** Compulsory for all IB Literature HL students across all 2 years; **ungraded** by IB, but subject to **IB audit for academic integrity/authentication** (especially for HL Essay & IO development).
* **Mandatory Exit Artifact:** **Works Studied Form**, logging every text, author, PRL period/region/form, AoE placement, and which single assessment (`IO`, `HLE`, `Paper 2`) each text was assigned to.
* **Recommended App Feature — Digital Learner Portfolio Summary Card per Work:**
  - Metadata (`title`, `author`, `translator`, `literary_form`, `prl_period`, `prl_region`, `continent`, `original_vs_translation`, `prl_vs_free_choice`)
  - AoE & Concept Tag Matrix (all 7 concepts mapped with 1–2 concrete textual hooks)
  - Key Passages Index (`<= 40 lines` bookmarks for IO)
  - Assessment Lock State (`unassigned` | `IO` | `HL_Essay` | `Paper_2_Prep`)

### 2.2 The 7 Central Concepts (Conceptual Inquiry Framework)

Although not graded as standalone rubric lines, the **7 Central Concepts** organize inquiry across all 3 AoEs and serve as the primary springboard for formulating a sharp **HL Essay Line of Inquiry**.

| Concept | Operational Definition in Literary Study | Key Analytical Questions for Students | HL Essay Line of Inquiry Archetype |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Identity** | How texts construct, fracture, perform, or contest individual and collective identities (gender, race, class, nation, selfhood), and how reader identity shapes reading. | Whose identity is centered or silenced? How do language, naming, or setting construct selfhood? Is identity depicted as fluid or fixed? | *"How does [Author] use [device/structure] in [Work] to represent the fracturing of [group/character] identity under [context]?"* |
| **2. Culture** | How social environments, traditions, rituals, beliefs, and power hierarchies shape both the text's production and its reception across cultures. | Does the text reinforce or critique dominant cultural norms? How do cultural idioms/proverbs/taboos function formally? How does reception shift across cultural borders? | *"To what extent does [Author] deploy [cultural motif/voice] to expose the moral hypocrisy of [specific cultural code] in [Work]?"* |
| **3. Creativity** | How writers exercise imagination, aesthetic innovation, and formal experimentation (or subvert inherited genre rules), and how reading is itself a creative act. | How does the writer manipulate form, fragmentation, metafiction, or imagery? Where does the text break genre conventions, and to what thematic end? | *"Through what [metafictional/prosodic/structural] innovations does [Author] subvert [convention] in [Work] to reflect [thematic idea]?"* |
| **4. Communication** | The dynamic triangle of writer–text–reader: how writers establish persona, manipulate tone, anticipate audience assumptions, or portray communication breakdown. | Who is the implied audience? How are silence, ellipsis, censorship, or unreliable narration used to distort or withhold communication? | *"How does [Author] utilize [unreliable narration/silence/rhetorical irony] in [Work] to depict the failure of authentic human communication?"* |
| **5. Perspective** | The viewpoints, focalization, biases, and ideological lenses through which characters, narrators, and readers perceive truth and events. | Whose perspective dominates? Whose is marginalized? How do shifts in focalization or temporal distance alter the reader's moral judgment? | *"In what ways does shifting [focalization/epistolary framing] in [Work] manipulate reader sympathy and destabilize moral certainty?"* |
| **6. Transformation** | Structural, psychological, or societal metamorphosis; intertextual adaptation of earlier myths/genres; and the text's power to transform the reader. | How do characters, settings, or motifs evolve or degenerate across the arc? How does the work transform an earlier literary source or tradition? | *"How does [Author] chart the psychological transformation of [character] through recurring shifts in [imagery/setting/syntax]?"* |
| **7. Representation** | How language and artistic form actively *construct* (rather than passively mirror) reality, historical trauma, marginalized groups, or abstract concepts. | What is stylized, foregrounded, or omitted in representing reality? How does aesthetic form shape political or ethical truth? | *"How does [Author] employ [expressionistic staging/magical realism/parody] in [Work] to represent the trauma of [phenomenon]?"* |

### 2.3 The 4 Literary Forms & Genre Conventions

Paper 1 HL guarantees **two different literary forms**, and Paper 2 / IO / HL Essay require **form-sensitive analysis** (analyzing *how* the unique affordances of prose, poetry, or drama create meaning).

#### Form 1: Prose — Fiction (Novels, Novellas, Short Stories, Graphic Fiction)
* **Narrative Architecture:**
  - *Point of View & Focalization:* First-person autodiegetic/homodiegetic (often unreliable, retrospective, or confessional), third-person omniscient vs. limited focalization, second-person ("you" direct address/implication), alternating multiple narrators, epistolary/frame narratives.
  - *Free Indirect Discourse (FID):* Blending third-person narrator voice with a character's internal idiom/consciousness without quotation marks — creating simultaneous intimacy and ironic distance.
  - *Stream of Consciousness & Interior Monologue:* Associative, fragmented syntax mirroring unfiltered psychological flow.
* **Temporality & Structure:** Linear vs. non-linear/fragmented chronology, *in medias res*, prolepsis (flashforward), analepsis (flashback), pacing/narrative duration (scene vs. summary vs. ellipsis), cyclical endings vs. open/ambiguous denouements, chapter/part divisions.
* **Characterization & World-Building:** Direct vs. indirect characterization, character foils, dialogue vs. internal thought, setting as pathetic fallacy or social chronotope (confined domestic spaces, borderlands, dystopian architecture), recurring motifs and symbolic objects.

#### Form 2: Prose — Non-Fiction (Essays, Memoirs, Autobiographies, Speeches, Letters, Travel Writing, Graphic Memoir)
* **Voice, Persona & Ethos:** The constructed literary "I" (memoirist/essayist persona vs. historical author), retrospective self-interrogation (the older narrating self judging the younger experiencing self), intimacy vs. polemical authority.
* **Rhetorical & Persuasive Architecture:** Classical appeals (*ethos*, *pathos*, *logos*), anecdote-to-thesis movement, counter-argument and concession (*procatalepsis*), direct address, rhetorical questions, anaphora, tricolon, epistrophe, antithesis.
* **Hybrid Structural Pacing:** Blending lyrical description, historical exposition, philosophical digression, and reconstructed dialogue; epigraphs and paratext; tonal shifts (elegiac, satirical, urgent, clinical).
* **Graphic Memoir Conventions (e.g., *Persepolis*, *Maus*, *Fun Home*):** When taught as Literature (PRL Prose Non-fiction), analysis MUST integrate **visual-verbal grammar**: panel size/transitions (closure), gutters (unseen violence/time), splash pages, monochrome contrast/chiaroscuro, speech balloons vs. narrative captions (voice-over vs. dialogue dissonance), facial stylization/iconography.

#### Form 3: Poetry (Lyric, Sonnet, Dramatic Monologue, Elegy, Ode, Ballad, Free Verse, Verse Novel)
* **Speaker vs. Poet Distinction:** Always distinguish the **speaker / persona** (especially in dramatic monologues like Duffy's *The World's Wife* or Browning) from the biographical poet.
* **Prosody, Meter & Rhythm:** Iambic pentameter, trochaic, anapestic, dactylic, spondaic substitutions, alexandrine, ballad meter, free verse (*vers libre*) cadence, rhythmic disruption mirroring psychological fracture.
* **Lineation & Pause:** **Enjambment** (momentum, double meaning across line breaks, syntactic suspension), **end-stopped lines** (closure, certainty, rigidity), **caesura** (mid-line pause via punctuation creating hesitation, fracture, or dramatic pivot).
* **Stanzaic & Traditional Forms:** Petrarchan vs. Shakespearean sonnet (and **volta** / tonal-argumentative turn at line 9 or 13), villanelle (obsessive refrain), sestina, ghazal, haiku, couplets (heroic vs. open), tercets, quatrains, concrete/visual spacing on the page.
* **Sound & Lexical Texture:** Alliteration, assonance, consonance, sibilance, plosives, fricatives, onomatopoeia, internal rhyme, slant/half-rhyme (dissonance/unresolved tension), regular rhyme scheme (closure/predictability), polysemy, ambiguity.

#### Form 4: Drama (Tragedy, Comedy, Naturalism/Realism, Absurdism, Epic Theatre, Expressionism)
* **Text vs. Performance ("Script as Blueprint"):** Examiners specifically reward students who treat drama as **performed theatre**, not just spoken prose!
* **Stagecraft & Non-Verbal Elements:**
  - *Stage Directions:* Parenthetical character behaviors, entrance/exit choreography, silence/pause cues (e.g., Pinter's pauses, Chekhovian silences).
  - *Proxemics & Blocking:* Physical distance between characters signaling power, intimacy, alienation, or territorial control; vertical/horizontal stage positioning (upstage/downstage).
  - *Set, Lighting, Sound & Props:* Expressionistic lighting/music (e.g., Williams's colored paper lantern and Varsouviana polka), symbolic props (e.g., Nora's tarantella costume/Christmas tree), diegetic vs. non-diegetic sound, domestic claustrophobia vs. off-stage unseen worlds.
* **Dramatic Dialogue & Irony:** **Dramatic irony** (audience knows what characters do not), **soliloquy** vs. **monologue** vs. **aside**, **stichomythia** (rapid-fire alternating lines signaling conflict/escalation), subtext (what is unsaid beneath polite speech), interruptions and overlapping dialogue.
* **Structural & Genre Mechanics:** Classical Aristotelian unities & tragic structure (hamartia, peripeteia, anagnorisis, catharsis, chorus), 3-act/5-act structure, fourth-wall realism vs. Brechtian alienation (*Verfremdungseffekt*), Theatre of the Absurd circularity.

### 3.1 Literary Device Matrix (With Feature-Spotting vs. Effect-Driven Templates)

> **Core Pedagogical Engine:** IB examiners report that **Criterion B inflation** (rewarding students who merely name devices without evaluating *how* they shape meaning) is the #1 grading pitfall. Every flashcard, drill, and AI feedback prompt in Simple Revision should enforce the **Device → Mechanism → Effect → Deeper Meaning** chain.

#### Category A: Figurative Language & Imagery

| Device | Definition | Example | Feature-Spotting (Band 2 ❌) | Effect-Driven Analysis (Band 5 ✅) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Extended Metaphor / Conceit** | Sustaining a single figurative comparison across multiple lines, stanzas, or chapters | The city mapped as a diseased body | *"The writer uses an extended metaphor comparing the city to a human body."* | *"By mapping urban infrastructure onto biological anatomy ('veins', 'gangrenous alleys'), the narrator naturalizes socio-economic decay as an organic pathology beyond individual moral reform."* |
| **Symbolism** | Concrete object, color, or setting carrying layered abstract significance | Gatsby's green light; Nora's macaroons | *"The green light is a symbol of Gatsby's dreams and the American Dream."* | *"Positioned across an unbridgeable bay and flickering only through fog, the green light materializes the paradox of aspirational desire: an aestheticized beacon whose radiance depends on permanent distance."* |
| **Pathetic Fallacy / Ecological Mirror** | Attributing human emotion or psychological states to weather and landscape | Storm during *King Lear*'s madness | *"Shakespeare uses pathetic fallacy with the storm to match Lear's angry mood."* | *"The violent tempest externalizes Lear's cognitive disintegration, stripping away royal ceremony until sovereign authority is indistinguishable from exposed animal vulnerability."* |
| **Synecdoche & Metonymy** | Substituting a part for the whole (synecdoche) or an associated attribute (metonymy) | *"The Crown"* for monarchy; *"a pair of ragged claws"* | *"Eliot uses synecdoche with 'ragged claws' to describe Prufrock."* | *"Reducing the speaker to 'a pair of ragged claws' fragments human agency into animalistic reflex, enacting Prufrock's acute self-estrangement and paralysis."* |

| Device | Definition | Example | Feature-Spotting (Band 2 ❌) | Effect-Driven Analysis (Band 5 ✅) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Parataxis vs. Hypotaxis** | Short, coordinate/unlinked clauses (parataxis) vs. nested subordinate clauses (hypotaxis) | Hemingway / Camus vs. Henry James / Woolf | *"Camus uses short sentences throughout Chapter 1."* | *"Relentless parataxis strips away causal conjunctions ('because', 'therefore'), projecting a flat emotional landscape in which grief and bureaucratic routine carry identical weight."* |

#### Category D: Structural & Narrative Architecture

| Device | Definition | Example | Feature-Spotting (Band 2 ❌) | Effect-Driven Analysis (Band 5 ✅) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Free Indirect Discourse (FID)** | Third-person narration infused with a character's internal vocabulary/syntax without speech tags | *"She would never forgive him, never—how dare he?"* | *"The narrator knows what the character is thinking."* | *"Slipping seamlessly into FID collapses the boundary between objective narrator and self-deluding protagonist, inviting simultaneous empathy and ironic scrutiny."* |

#### Category E: Dramatic & Stagecraft Conventions

| Device | Definition | Example | Feature-Spotting (Band 2 ❌) | Effect-Driven Analysis (Band 5 ✅) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

### 3.2 Global Issue Bank (5 Fields of Inquiry + 28 Cross-Text Pairings)

#### Official Rules for Validating an IB Global Issue
Every Individual Oral (IO) global issue MUST satisfy **all three official IB criteria**:
1. **Significance on a wide/large scale:** It affects substantial populations or reflects major structural/human dynamics (not an isolated character squabble).
2. **Transnational:** It crosses national, geographic, and cultural boundaries (even if explored through locally specific texts).
3. **Impact is felt in everyday local contexts:** It manifests concretely in daily human lives, families, communities, or interpersonal interactions (not pure abstract geopolitics).

> **Broad Theme → Narrowed Valid Global Issue Rule:**  
> ❌ *Bad (Too Broad):* `"Gender"` or `"Power"` or `"Climate Change"`  
> ❌ *Bad (Too Local/Text-Bound):* `"The oppression of women in 19th-century Norway"`  
> ✅ *Good (Focused, Transnational, Everyday):* `"The ways in which patriarchal economic dependency erodes female autonomy within the domestic sphere."`

#### The 5 Official Fields of Inquiry
1. **Culture, identity and community** *(family, class, race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, gender, sexuality, migration, colonialism, nationalism)*
2. **Beliefs, values and education** *(value systems, religious dogma, formal/informal education, ethics, generational ideological conflict)*
3. **Politics, power and justice** *(human rights, state authoritarianism, judicial bias, resistance, wealth inequality, war/peace, censorship)*
4. **Art, creativity and the imagination** *(aesthetic expression, function/commodification of art, storytelling as survival or propaganda, censorship of artists)*
5. **Science, technology and the environment** *(human–nature relationship, ecological degradation, technological surveillance, bioethics, alienation)*

*(Note: Students may define a global issue outside these 5 fields, provided it meets the 3 core criteria.)*

### 3.3 Paper 1: Guided Literary Analysis Framework (HL)

#### Exam Parameters & Time Budget (2 hours 15 minutes | 40 Marks | 35% HL Weight)
- **Format:** Two unseen literary passages from **two different literary forms** (drawn from Prose: Fiction, Prose: Non-fiction, Poetry, Drama). Each passage has **one optional guiding question**.
- **HL Mandate:** You MUST write **two separate, independent essays** (one on Text A, one on Text B). **Do NOT compare Text A and Text B.**
- **Recommended Time Allocation (~67.5 mins per passage):**
  1. **0–5m (Global Triage):** Skim both passages & guiding questions. Choose order (either *stronger text first* to bank momentum, or *harder text first* while mentally fresh — train one consistent habit).
  2. **Per Passage (~65 mins):**
     - `10m`: Close read twice; annotate macro-shifts (beginning/middle/end or volta), narrative/poetic voice, form-specific conventions, and micro-devices (diction, syntax, imagery, sound, stagecraft).
     - `5m`: Plan thesis + 3–4 analytical claims (avoid rigid 5-paragraph shoe-horning if the text moves through 2 or 4 phases).
     - `45m`: Write essay with dense embedded textual quotes and explicit effect analysis.
     - `5m`: Proofread for Criterion D precision, analytical verbs, and complete balance.

#### Guiding Question Decoding Protocol
1. **Is the Guiding Question Mandatory?** **No** — the official IB rubric does NOT score whether you answered the guiding question directly. However, **95%+ of guiding questions pinpoint the text's richest formal engine and central thematic tension**, making them ideal thesis scaffolds.
2. **Two-Part Anatomy:** Almost every IB Literature Paper 1 guiding question splits into:
   - **Form / Method Focus:** *"How does the poet use structural shifts and sensory imagery..."* / *"How do stage directions and dialogue..."*
   - **Meaning / Effect Target:** *"...to convey the speaker's conflicted nostalgia?"* / *"...expose shifting power dynamics?"*
3. **Rule of Expansion:** Never restrict your essay *only* to the single technique named in the guiding question. Anchor on it, then demonstrate how **complementary features** (e.g., syntax, tone shifts, prosody, proxemics) reinforce or complicate that effect.

#### Structural Blueprints (Form-Adaptive!)
- **Option A — Chronological / Movement-Led Structure** *(Best for Poetry with a volta, dramatic scenes with escalating tension, or short narrative arcs)*:
  - **Intro:** Context of passage (speaker/situation/form), overarching tonal arc, sharp **thesis statement** addressing *how* and *why* the passage evolves.
  - **Body 1 (Opening / Establishment):** How initial voice, setting, or stanzaic order establishes baseline tension.
  - **Body 2 (Pivot / Escalation / Volta):** How structural, syntactic, or dramatic turning points fracture or intensify that tension.
  - **Body 3 (Resolution / Anti-Closure):** How final images, sound patterns, or stage directions leave the reader/audience positioned.
  - **Conclusion:** Synthesize the passage's overarching aesthetic/thematic achievement without repeating topic sentences.
- **Option B — Macro-to-Micro Thematic / Concept-Led Structure** *(Best for dense descriptive prose fiction, discursive non-fiction essays, or multi-faceted lyric poems)*:
  - **Intro:** Situate passage + overarching thesis linking 2–3 key authorial strategies to complex meaning.
  - **Body 1 (Macro-Structure & Perspective/Voice):** Narrative framing, focalization, stanzaic form, or rhetorical progression.
  - **Body 2 (Imagery, Symbolism & Figurative Texture):** Semantic fields, motifs, sensory contrasts.
  - **Body 3 (Diction, Syntax & Sound/Stagecraft):** Granular linguistic/sonic/dramatic choices reinforcing or undercutting surface claims.
  - **Conclusion:** Final evaluative synthesis of the author's craft.

### 3.4 Paper 2: Comparative Essay Framework (HL)

#### Exam Parameters & Time Budget (1 hour 45 minutes | Closed-Book | 25% HL Weight)
- **Format:** 4 general literature questions; choose **1**. Compare **2 studied works** (neither may be used in the IO or HL Essay).
- **Closed-Book Evidence Standard:** You are **NOT** penalized for not memorizing verbatim block quotes, though accurate brief micro-quotes (`2–6 words`), exact character/scene references, structural arcs, and formal details (e.g., *stage directions*, *narrative shifts*, *recurring symbols*) are essential to reach Bands 4–5 in Criteria A & B1.
- **Recommended Time Allocation (105 mins):**
  - `10m`: Unpack all 4 prompts; pick the best prompt–text match.
  - `15m`: Draft comparative thesis + 3–4 integrated thematic/formal axes (ABABAB plan) + recalled textual moments/micro-quotes.
  - `70m`: Write comparative essay.
  - `10m`: Proofread for balanced treatment, analytical register, and prompt alignment.

#### Comparative Thesis Formula & ABABAB Structure
* **Weak Parallel Thesis (Caps B2 at Band 2 ❌):** *"Both A Doll's House and The Handmaid's Tale explore women's oppression through symbolism and setting."*
* **Strong Evaluative Comparative Thesis (Targets B2 Band 5 ✅):** *"While both Ibsen and Atwood expose how patriarchal institutions reduce female agency within confined domestic spaces, Ibsen deploys naturalist parlor realism to build toward an individual, defiant rupture of bourgeois marriage, whereas Atwood's fragmented retrospective monologue traps the protagonist inside a totalizing linguistic regime where resistance remains clandestine and historically uncertain."*
* **Paragraph Architecture (`ABABAB` Integrated Synthesis — NEVER `AAA BBB` Block Essays):**
  - Each body paragraph explores **one shared comparative axis** (e.g., *Spatial Confinement*, *Control of Language*, *Ambiguous Endings*), analyzing Text A (`B1`), explicitly pivoting via comparative connective (`whereas`, `conversely`, `similarly, yet through...`), analyzing Text B (`B1`), and **synthesizing what the contrast reveals about the prompt (`B2`)**.
* **May 2026+ "And/Or" B2 Rule:** You do NOT have to artificially force equal counts of similarities and differences; you may lean primarily on similarities, primarily on contrasts, or a synthesis of both — whichever produces the deepest critical insight.

### 3.5 IO & HL Essay Studio Specs (for Simple Revision)

#### A. Individual Oral (IO) Prep Studio Workflow
1. **Work Pairing & Language Validation Gate:**
   - User selects `Work 1` (must have metadata `original_language == 'English'`) and `Work 2` (must have metadata `is_translation == true`).
   - System verifies neither work is locked to `HL_Essay` or `Paper_2_Prep`.

   - Tag to 1 of the **5 Fields of Inquiry** (or custom).
   - Automated check flags single-word themes (`"Power"`, `"Identity"`) or single-country historical labels (`"Women in 1920s America"`) and prompts the student to rewrite into an active tension meeting: **Wide-scale**, **Transnational**, and **Everyday Local Impact**.
3. **Extract Selector & Validator (2 Extracts):**
   - Each extract must be **continuous** (no `[...]` ellipses or splicing two poems/chapters together).
   - **Hard Limit:** `<= 40 lines` each (warn at >40 lines; block or flag non-compliance). For poetry, an entire short poem `<= 40 lines` counts as the extract while still requiring discussion of the wider `15–20 poem` collection!
   - Clean copy mode (strips student annotations during timed rehearsal).
4. **10-Bullet Outline Builder:**
   - Hard cap: **10 bullet points maximum** on **1 page**.
   - Soft/advised limit: **<= 300 words total** (~25–30 words/bullet); flags full prose paragraphs ("Script Warning: convert full sentences into memory prompts").
   - Recommended timing split baked into UI:
     - Bullets 1–2 (`~1:00`): Global Issue definition + introduce both works/extracts (+ translator name) + central claim.
     - Bullets 3–5 (`~4:00`): Work 1 (`~2m extract close-reading` + `~2m wider work`).
     - Bullets 6–8 (`~4:00`): Work 2 (`~2m extract close-reading` + `~2m wider work`).
     - Bullets 9–10 (`~1:00`): Synthesis of how both works illuminate the Global Issue *(Remember: IO prompt does NOT require forced comparison/contrast between texts, though brief synthesis strengthens cohesion!)*.
5. **Dual-Timer Oral Simulator + AI Teacher Q&A (5 Minutes):**
   - `10:00` countdown timer with pace checkpoints (`1:00`, `5:00`, `9:00`).
   - Audio/transcript capture followed by **`5:00` interactive teacher questioning**: AI asks targeted follow-up questions probing gaps (e.g., missed wider-work evidence, unanalyzed form/translator choices, or unexamined counter-perspectives in the extract).

#### B. HL Essay Studio Workflow
1. **Work Eligibility Lock:** Select 1 studied work not used in IO or Paper 2. If selecting a collection (poetry/short stories/essays), record which subset of texts represents the unified work.
2. **Concept & Line of Inquiry (LoI) Architect:**
   - Pick 1 primary anchor from the **7 Central Concepts** (optional by IB rules, strongly recommended).
   - Transform broad topic into a tight `How` / `To what extent` / `In what ways` LoI naming **Author + Work + Literary Feature(s) + Concept/Purpose**.
3. **Live Word-Count Engine (`1,200–1,500` words):**
   - **Included:** Introduction, body paragraphs, conclusion, **and all in-text/block quotations**.
   - **Excluded:** Title page, line of inquiry header, bibliography/Works Cited, and footnotes containing *only* bibliographic citations.
   - **Guardrail Alert:** Hard warning at `< 1,200` (underdeveloped) and `> 1,500` (examiners stop reading at word 1,500!), plus a footnote analyzer warning against stuffing commentary into footnotes.
4. **Drafting & Citation Checker:** Tracks MLA/Chicago consistency, quote integration (no "dropped" unanalyzed block quotes), and sustained focus on the LoI across every topic sentence.

Supporting examples **well integrated** into sentence and paragraph structure. | Language is **very clear, effective, carefully chosen and precise**, with a high degree of accuracy; register and style **effective and appropriate** to the task. |

#### AI Grader Calibration Notes (Preventing Automated Scoring Drift)
1. **Paper 1 vs. Paper 2 Contamination Guard:** Never evaluate Paper 1 responses with comparative B2 logic, and never award Paper 2 **B2** marks merely for alternating mentions of two works without genuine synthesis of similarities/differences.
2. **Criterion B Inflation Guard:** An essay listing 5 accurate literary devices *without* explaining how each device shapes meaning must be capped at **Band 2–3** on Criterion B / B1.
3. **Criterion C Formula Guard:** Do not auto-award Band 5 on Criterion C for a generic 5-paragraph essay if the structure fights the literary text's actual movement (e.g., breaking a continuous lyric arc into arbitrary isolated device buckets).

#### 8 Representative Worked Quote Flashcards (Across Forms & Translations)

1. ***Macbeth* — William Shakespeare (Drama | Original English | 16th–17th c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather / The multitudinous seas incarnadine"` (Act 2, Scene 2)
   - **Speaker & Context:** Macbeth immediately after Duncan's regicide, contrasting his terror with Lady Macbeth's pragmatic `"A little water clears us of this deed"`.
   - **Devices:** Rhetorical question, classical hyperbole (*Neptune's ocean*), Latinate polysyllabic diction (*multitudinous*, *incarnadine*) contrasted with monosyllabic Anglo-Saxon (*wash*, *clean*, *red*), blood motif.
   - **Effect Analysis:** The abrupt syntactic shift from expansive Latinate polysyllables to stark monosyllabic dread exposes Macbeth's immediate recognition that moral transgression is irreversible and contagious—transforming cosmic nature rather than being cleansed by it.
   - **Tags:** AoE: `ReadersWritersTexts`, `TimeAndSpace` | Concepts: `Transformation`, `Identity` | GI: *Moral corruption of unchecked political ambition*.

2. ***A Doll's House* — Henrik Ibsen, tr. Michael Meyer (Drama | Translation | 19th c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"Is my little lark twittering out there?" ... "I have been your doll-wife, just as at home I was Papa's doll-child."` (Act 1 / Act 3)
   - **Speaker & Context:** Torvald's opening diminutives juxtaposed against Nora's Act 3 awakening before slamming the front door.
   - **Devices:** Zoomorphic/infantilizing diminutives (*little lark*), symmetrical parallel syntax (*doll-wife* / *doll-child*), naturalistic stage exit (auditory offstage door slam).
   - **Effect Analysis:** Echoing Torvald's opening animal endearments through razor-sharp syntactic parallelism, Nora strips patriarchal affection of sentimental camouflage, re-framing domestic marriage as generational infantilization before breaking realist comic resolution.
   - **Tags:** AoE: `TimeAndSpace`, `Intertextuality` | Concepts: `Identity`, `Culture`, `Transformation` | GI: *Patriarchal domestic dependency*.

3. ***The Great Gatsby* — F. Scott Fitzgerald (Prose Fiction | Original English | 20th c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."` (Chapter 9)
   - **Speaker & Context:** Nick Carraway's closing elegiac reflection after Gatsby's sparsely attended funeral.
   - **Devices:** First-person plural inclusive pronoun (*we*), maritime metaphor (*boats against the current*), passive participle (*borne back*), sibilance & rhythmic trochaic drag (*ceaselessly*).
   - **Effect Analysis:** Shifting from retrospective chronicle into collective pronoun *we*, Nick universalizes Gatsby's delusion into a national tragedy, while the passive construction *borne back* denies human agency against historical determinism.
   - **Tags:** AoE: `ReadersWritersTexts`, `TimeAndSpace` | Concepts: `Perspective`, `Representation` | GI: *Illusion and class exclusion in aspirational consumer cultures*.

4. ***Beloved* — Toni Morrison (Prose Fiction | Original English | 20th c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"It was not a story to pass on."` (Epilogue)
   - **Speaker & Context:** Final haunting refrain following Beloved's disappearance and the community's effort to heal Sethe.
   - **Devices:** Polysemy / syntactic ambiguity (*"pass on"* = transmit vs. walk away from vs. die), tonal understatement, rhythmic cadence.
   - **Effect Analysis:** Morrison exploits the dual meaning of *pass on* to trap the reader in ethical paradox: the trauma of chattel slavery is simultaneously too unbearable to repeat and too catastrophic to forget.
   - **Tags:** AoE: `TimeAndSpace`, `Intertextuality` | Concepts: `Communication`, `Representation`, `Culture` | GI: *Intergenerational suppression vs. witnessing of historical trauma*.

5. ***Chronicle of a Death Foretold* — Gabriel García Márquez, tr. Gregory Rabassa (Prose Fiction | Translation | 20th c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"There had never been a death more foretold."` (Chapter 3)
   - **Speaker & Context:** The investigative narrator summarizing why the entire town failed to warn Santiago Nasar despite the Vicario brothers announcing their murder plan everywhere.
   - **Devices:** Structural prolepsis, paradoxical superlative, detached pseudo-journalistic register, ironic understatement.
   - **Effect Analysis:** Framing communal negligence in deadpan reportorial syntax implicates the entire town's fatalistic honor culture, shifting narrative suspense from plot outcome to collective moral cowardice.
   - **Tags:** AoE: `TimeAndSpace`, `Intertextuality` | Concepts: `Culture`, `Perspective`, `Communication` | GI: *Collective bystander complicity in honor-based violence*.

6. ***Persepolis* — Marjane Satrapi, tr. Mattias Ripa & Blake Ferris (Prose Non-fiction / Graphic Memoir | Translation | 21st c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"In 1980, it became obligatory to wear the veil at school."` + Panel of identical veiled schoolgirls mirrored by *The Veil* opening chapter.
   - **Speaker & Context:** Young Marji narrating post-Revolution cultural homogenization in Tehran.
   - **Devices:** Visual-verbal irony (monochrome visual uniformity vs. playful child speech bubbles), high-contrast chiaroscuro, first-person retrospective caption vs. child focalization.
   - **Effect Analysis:** Contrasting stark black-and-white visual cloning with the individualized, mischievous voice of children exposes how authoritarian uniformity clashes with irrepressible personal identity.
   - **Tags:** AoE: `TimeAndSpace`, `ReadersWritersTexts` | Concepts: `Identity`, `Representation`, `Perspective` | GI: *State regulation of bodily autonomy and ideological schooling*.

7. ***"Havisham"* (from *Mean Time*) / *"Medusa"* (from *The World's Wife*) — Carol Ann Duffy (Poetry | Original English | 20th c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"Beloved sweetheart bastard. Not a day since then / I haven't wished him dead."` (*"Havisham"*, lines 1–2)
   - **Speaker & Context:** Dramatic monologue reimagining Dickens's jilted Miss Havisham.
   - **Devices:** Oxymoron (*sweetheart bastard*), caesura (sentence-stop mid-line), dramatic monologue subverting canonical intertext, double negative.
   - **Effect Analysis:** Colliding romantic endearment with bitter invective inside an opening three-word oxymoron fractures traditional Victorian victimhood, transforming repressed grief into ferocious linguistic agency.
   - **Tags:** AoE: `Intertextuality`, `ReadersWritersTexts` | Concepts: `Transformation`, `Creativity`, `Identity` | GI: *Reclaiming silenced female voices from patriarchal literary canons*.

8. ***Selected Poems* (*"View with a Grain of Sand"* / *"Nothing Twice"*) — Wisława Szymborska, tr. Stanisław Barańczak & Clare Cavanagh (Poetry | Translation | 20th c.)**
   - **Quote:** `"We call it a grain of sand, / but it calls itself neither grain nor sand."` (*"View with a Grain of Sand"*)
   - **Speaker & Context:** Philosophical lyric questioning anthropocentric naming and human projection onto nature.
   - **Devices:** Antithesis, chiasmus-like lexical reversal, philosophical understatement, personification negated (*calls itself neither*).
   - **Effect Analysis:** Exposing human nouns as arbitrary projections rather than ontological truth decenters human ego, gently critiquing ideological certainty (a hallmark of Szymborska's anti-totalitarian irony).
   - **Tags:** AoE: `ReadersWritersTexts`, `TimeAndSpace` | Concepts: `Communication`, `Perspective`, `Representation` | GI: *Anthropocentrism and the limits of human language*.
